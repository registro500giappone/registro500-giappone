Nothing lights up when you turn the key

You turn the key ON and not a single warning light on the instrument panel comes on. We follow the electrical path on a single diagram and narrow down where it stops.

This story is about the FIAT 500 (D, F, L and R models). The wiring is drawn from the original 500 sources.

The key is ON, yet neither GENERAT. (charging) nor OLIO (oil pressure) lights up. That's the symptom on this page.

Before you get the tester out, one thing Pull the starter lever and see whether the starter turns. That alone halves the suspects (① in the narrowing-down table below).
If a tool on the battery's positive (+) terminal touches the body, enough current flows in that instant to burn the tool. There is no fuse on this heavy cable. Before touching terminals or heavy cables, disconnect the battery's negative (−) terminal first.
⚠️ While you are looking for the cause, don't leave the key ON. If the contact points have stopped closed, current keeps flowing through the ignition coil and points and they heat up (if they stopped open, nothing flows). You can't see from outside which way they stopped, and the battery keeps draining meanwhile. Turn the key back to OFF when you walk away from the car.
How it works

Before the warning lights come on with the key ON,The power makes a round trip of the car

From here on, "heavy cable" refers to the fact that the original wiring diagram draws only this supply section thicker than the rest. It is the section that bypasses the fuses and carries large currents.

  1. The heavy red cable from the battery's positive terminal splits in two along the way. This is not a screw terminal but a joint between wires. Straight down leads to the contact on top of the starter motor (Part 3); this story follows the other one.
  2. From the fork, a heavy brown cable runs to the back of the car and arrives at regulator terminal 30. From there a heavy red cable leaves the same terminal 30 and returns to the front — the incoming and outgoing cables share one terminal under one screw.
  3. ⚠️ Cars converted to an alternator don't have this "regulator terminal 30". The same role — the turning point where the heavy cable from the battery meets and heads back to the key — is taken on converted cars by the alternator's B+ terminal. Measure there instead (where the cables meet depends on how the conversion was done, so following the heavy cables on your own car by eye is the surest way). The shape of the path — "all the way to the back and then back again" — is the same on converted cars. What this turning point looks like on a converted car is drawn in the Part 1 extra, "The charge lamp won't go out: alternator conversion".
  4. It comes back to 30 on the fuse box (the supply side of fuse F1). From here a heavy red cable branches off to 30 on the ignition switch.
  5. Turn the key and 30 connects to 15/54; a single light blue wire goes to INTER in the instrument panel (= the one feed rail that supplies both warning lights; we'll use this name from here on). From there it splits to the two warning lights, which go down through terminal 51 (GENERAT.) and terminal 0 (OLIO) respectively.

The same "30" turns up in several places. It isn't the name of a part but a number for the terminal's role: 30 = a terminal that always has power regardless of the key, 15/54 = the terminal power leaves from when you turn the key (the original wiring diagram uses these numbers too). So the three 30s in the diagram are all on the same single path. In the diagram above, only the places numbered in the original have a tag — where there's no tag, it isn't a terminal.

Wire colours are the actual insulation colours on the car (ROSSO = red, MARRONE = brown, AZZURRO = light blue, VERDE = green, GRIGIO = grey). Yellow dots = where current is flowing now. Faded lines = where no power is reaching. Only the inside of the panel is drawn with thin, light lines, because its internal wiring can't be fully read from the original diagram (an estimate); this sets it apart from the certain lines.

A common question "If they're out while the engine is running, that's not a fault." In fact it's normal. GENERAT. goes out once charging starts, and OLIO goes out once oil pressure rises (the Part 1 and Part 2 stories). This page only deals with no lights with the key ON and the engine stopped. If the engine starts, the surest check is two steps: "they come on with the key ON, and go out once it starts".
Why it can be narrowed down

If both are out,The culpritcan only be in "the part both share"

In the original wiring diagram, the panel warning lights are all fed by the same single light blue wire (INTER); they differ only in where they go to ground. So:

  • Both went out → the shared part (battery – key – INTER, plus the ground on the return path) is suspect.
  • Only one went out → the shared part is in the clear. Only where that warning light goes to ground is suspect (= the Part 1 and Part 2 stories).

Above, only the grey wire going down from terminal 0 is broken (× mark). OLIO went out, but GENERAT. stays lit — proof that the shared part is alive. This one diagram is the basis for the narrowing-down above.

When something is wrong ①

One outbound wire broken =both go out

The diagram above shows the red wire to the ignition switch off (at the height of the red tag). Everything below that wire fades, and both went out. If the starter turned in ①, the candidates for the break are only inside the box:

  1. A flat battery or loose terminals (the most common). But then the starter won't turn either, so ① in the table splits it out.
  2. The heavy cable going to the back of the car / the one coming back, and their terminals off or loose. They meet at regulator terminal 30, so if that works loose, all the power at the front stops.
  3. The ignition switch itself (worn contacts, a key cylinder that turns without engaging).
  4. A break in the light blue wire to the instrument panel, or its connector pulled out.

It's tempting to suspect a fuse, but looking at the fuses won't fix this symptom. The red wire to the ignition switch branches off the supply side of the fuse (as the diagram shows, it doesn't pass through the fuse). The cross-check also confirms that "the warning lights come on even with F1 blown". It's exactly the same reason as in Part 1 (the charge lamp).

⭐ This red wire isn't the only thing that bypasses the fuses. The factory service standards list by name "the circuits not protected by fuses" — five on the D model = charging circuit and charge warning light / ignition / starting / oil pressure warning light / fuel reserve warning light. ⭐ On the L model this drops to three (charging circuit and charge warning light / ignition / starting), and the two warning lights moved inside the fuses.
In other words, much of what's involved in "the engine starts" and "the warning lights come on" sits outside the fuses. The range the fuse box can't tell you about differs this much between models.

When something is wrong ②

Return path broken =The wires have 12 V, yetNothing lights

This one shows the battery's negative terminal off. Look closely — the wires in the diagram are still coloured. So 12 V does get there. And still not one light comes on.

Current only flows round a complete loop. Even if the outbound path is alive, without a way back to the battery's negative terminal the loop doesn't close. A tester on the positive side reads a healthy 12 V, which makes it the most confusing form: "the power is there, but nothing works". Part 5 is the story with this return path as its subject.

Where it is on the car

The power reaching the keyruns all the way to the back of the car and returns

● The battery is at the front of the car (in the trunk), ● the regulator at the back (in the engine bay), and ● the fuse box, ● the key and ● the speedometer are back at the front. A path that looked like a straight line in the diagram makes a round trip of the real car. That's how "one loose terminal at the back kills the whole instrument panel at the front" can happen.

Once fixed

I tightened the terminals.Both came on.

Power from the battery went round the back of the car, came back to the front, through the key to INTER in the panel, and the two warning lights went down to terminals 51 and 0, closing the loop. Now start the engine. If both go out, charging and oil pressure are both fine.

⭐ When you re-tighten, don't take the white, green or blue powder on the terminals for "insulation". The factory service manual says it conducts electricity — so it creates slow leakage paths not only between terminal and cable, but between the terminals and between the cells. This is one reason a battery goes flat when the car isn't being driven.
⛔ Don't put grease on after cleaning it off (it reacts with the electrolyte and makes the same powder again). The factory specifies a thin coat of pure, sticky petroleum jelly. ⭐ And be especially thorough underneath the terminals — it builds up where you can't see.

And a word on the electrolyte. The level should be 3 mm above the separators (normally) to 5 mm (when fully charged); check it every 1,500 km and top up with distilled water only. The specific gravity in everyday use is 1.23–1.25 (all from the factory service manual). ⚠️ Don't try to correct the strength by adding dilute sulphuric acid — what's been lost is water.
