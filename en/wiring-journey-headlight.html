The headlights don't come on

A fault you notice after dark. — This circuit has something the seven parts so far didn't: low and high beams take different paths, and left and right are separate too. So just seeing what went out — right only, left only, low only, high only — almost pins down where the culprit is.

This story is about the FIAT 500 (D, F, L and R models). The wiring is drawn from the original 500 sources.

You switch on the light switch on the dash. You push the column lever down. And the front still stays dark — that's the symptom on this page. Check with the key turned (a 500's headlights work with the key turned right and in the parking-light position, turned left; the reason is explained below).

⚠️⚠️ This circuit can be live even with the key removed. In the parking-light position (key turned left) the key comes out, yet this wire still has power. So before touching the wiring, always disconnect the battery's negative (−) terminal. Removing the key alone doesn't cut it.
⚠️ Don't replace a fuse with a higher-rated one. On the 500 all six fuses are 8 amps; each low beam has its own fuse, left and right, and each high beam shares one with side lights. Raise the rating and the wire it was meant to protect burns first. If a fuse keeps blowing, first find out why (something rubbing through and shorting to ground).
⚠️ Don't drive at night, in tunnels or in rain with headlights that don't work. Even one side out is a roadworthiness failure. Let bulbs cool before touching them — right after use you can't hold them bare-handed.
How it works

From another branch of the ignition switch,through the light switch and column lever,and splits left and right

The stars of this circuit are two switches. Their jobs are completely different, so take a look at the real things first.

① is one of the small parts set into the dash; all you touch is a flat knob. Behind it is a tall black box with three wires plugged in at the back (shape based on a parts supplier's photo of the real thing). The one with steps is ②, the column lever, sticking out left of the steering wheel; the further down you push it, the further it goes I → II → III. These three positions aren't an inference: they're written as such in the factory owner's handbook (no. 12 in the 500D handbook). The solid line is where it is now, the dashed lines where it can go.

Let's work these two in the circuit diagram. Tap the light switch and the column lever right in the picture — “Off / On” on the dash, and “High · Side · Low” on the column lever. Each works on its own. ⭐ With the light switch off, moving the column lever changes nothing in the picture — that's the most important point of this part.

  1. The current comes from the battery to the ignition switch. Up to here it's the same path as in Parts 1 and 4.
  2. ⭐ But the ignition switch has two sets of contacts inside. One closes only when the key is turned right (the branch to ignition, warning lights and fuse F2). The other closes both when turned right and in the parking-light position turned left, and from it a black wire heads to the light switch on the dash. — That's why, in the parking-light position, the key comes out but the headlight wire stays live.
  3. 1The light switch on the dash (the original calls it the exterior lighting switch). ⭐ As soon as it's on, the side lights and number plate light come on — because the green wire branches off here to fuses F5 and F6. And at the same time, power reaches the column lever along the white wire.
  4. 2⭐ The column lever (the exterior lighting changeover switch in the original). Its job is to pass the power arriving on the white wire to the low side or the high side. ⭐ At I, it passes it to neither.
  5. ⭐ The low side becomes a single grey/red wire into the fuse box and splits inside the box into F3 (right) and F4 (left). From there on, left and right are completely independent.
  6. ⭐ The high side is two wires, green/black (left) and green (right), going straight from the column lever to the headlights — unlike the low side, no fuse splits them left and right beyond this point. ⚠️ Still, they aren't unprotected. The high-side power fed to the column lever comes from beyond fuses F5 and F6 (F5 left, F6 right; → note ② at the end). ⭐ And from the left wire, one more branches off to the instrument panel — the lamp that glows blue on high beam. We call it the “indicator”. This one wire will later cut the suspects in half.
  7. Each headlight has two filaments, one for low and one for high, in a single bulb, with one shared ground for the return.

Wire colours are the actual insulation colours on the car (ROSSO = red, MARRONE = brown, NERO = black, BIANCO = white, VERDE = green, GRIGIO-ROSSO = grey/red, VERDE E NERO = green/black). Yellow dots = where current is flowing now. Faint dashed lines are “branches this story doesn't take”; other stories live there.

Lights that can come on with the key out: not a fault The ignition switch has four terminals: 30 (from the battery), 15/54 (live when the key is turned right), and 30/4, of which there are two. The exterior lights take their power from this 30/4.
⭐ And the key has three positions. 0 = all off (the key comes out) /1 = turned right, the driving position /2 = Key turned left: parking (the key comes out here too). 30/4 is live in positions 1 and 2 and dead in position 0. In a footnote on “circuits live with the key in position 1 or 2”, the factory owner's handbook lists the high beam, low beam, flashing, side lights and number plate light.
— So “the headlights are on with the key out” does happen. But not “because they have nothing to do with the key”: it's because the key is still in position 2. The only things truly independent of the key are the horn and the interior light.
— This position 2 made sense for a design of 60 years ago. At night, after stopping the engine and parking, you could leave just the side lights on (European towns needed that back then). In today's use, though, forgetting to switch them off leads straight to a flat battery. Part 3 dealt with “the starter won't turn”, and the culprit can be in this story.
⚠️ All of this applies to cars with factory wiring. On cars modified to run the headlights through relays, they may be set up to go out when the key is removed.
Keep “on or off” and “where to” apart Even in the original legend the two have different names. The dash switch is Interruttore (= on/off switch: just on or off); the column lever is Commutatore (= changeover switch: where it goes). If the light switch isn't on, the lights won't stay on wherever you move the column lever. Conversely, if the light switch is on, only the side lights come on as long as the column lever is at I — if both the headlights and the side lights are out, suspect the light switch side first: that follows from this division of roles. ⭐If the side lights are on but the front stays dark, suspect the column lever and beyond. ⚠️There is one exception.Pull the column lever towards you (towards the wheel) and the low beam flashes on even with the light switch off — flashing. The 500D owner's handbook calls this part “exterior lighting changeover switch and headlight flasher” (Commutatore illuminazione esterna e lampi luce anabbaglianti), which shows that there is a separate path that bypasses the light switch. ⭐That path is drawn in the wiring diagram too — the column lever has one more supply wire that bypasses the light switch connected to it (terminal name in the original: INT). Flashing works regardless of the light switch because its power comes in by a different entrance altogether. ⛔However, how that wire is switched inside the lever isn't shown in the wiring diagram, so this diagram doesn't show flashing. ⭐What happens inside the lever is opened up all the way in the Part 8 extra “Inside the column lever”.
Narrowing it down

Read “what went out”from six combinations

Facing a wall, try both low and high. There are six combinations of what goes out, and each puts the culprit somewhere different. This is where this story is most useful.

The high-beam indicator (the blue one on the panel) is a clue too. On high, if the indicator lights but the left headlight doesn't, the culprit is the left bulb or ground (proof that power reaches the indicator). If the indicator goes out too, the culprit is further back — the left wire from the column lever, the column lever itself, or F5 (⭐ the indicator hangs off the same fuse as the left high beam).
⚠️ When both low beams go dim together, it may not be a break but voltage dropping from a poor contact on the ground side. This diagram can't deal with that (→ Part 5).

When something is wrong ①

Column lever at “I”:this is not a fault

The dash switch is on and the column lever is at I. The side lights and number plate light are on, and the white wire reaches the column lever. But the column lever passes the power to neither side, so the front stays dark — push the column lever to II and they come on.

This scene comes first because we'd like you to check it before panicking “they won't come on” in a dark car park. A 60-year-old column lever has shallow detents, and it's hard to feel that it has stopped at I — walk round to the front and see whether only the side lights are on.

When something is wrong ②

Low beam out on one side:that side's fuse

F4 (left) blown. The right low beam is on as if nothing happened; only the left is out. Up to the fuse box, the lit right side proves everything is in the clear.

⭐ Same F4 blown, column lever moved to high. The left high beam lights as if nothing happened — the high beam doesn't go through this fuse.

These two are the surest check before going to look at the fuses. “Only one side's low beam is out, and that side's high works” = the fuse. Conversely, if both low and high are out, the culprit isn't the fuse (it's the bulb or the ground).
⚠️ F3 is right, F4 is left. In the original fuse box, a single grey/red wire enters the 3rd fuse, and 3 and 4 are bridged inside the box — so one wire goes in, but left and right are independent coming out. Of the 500's six fuses, two are used only for the low beams. The layout shows the designers cared about “always keeping one eye open”.

When something is wrong ③

Same “left is out”, butthe indicatortells the culprits apart

The left headlight's ground is off (high beam). On the left, both low and high are out. Yet the blue indicator on the panel stays on.

This time the wire from the column lever to the left high beam is broken. The same “left won't light”, but the indicator went out too.

This is the high point of this story. The indicator branches off the left high-beam wire. So —
· Indicator on = the power reaches right up to the left light. The culprit is the bulb or the ground.
· Indicator off too = the power doesn't get that far. The culprit is the wire or the column lever.
Before you get the tester out, just looking at the instrument panel halves the suspects. ⚠️ But the indicator only watches the left side. it lights happily even with the right high beam out — the indicator is no guarantee that “the right is on too”.

When something is wrong ④

White wire broken:all four go out

The white wire between the light switch and the column lever is broken. Low, high, left, right — all four go out. Yet the side lights stay on (the green wire branches off before this point).

⭐ “All the headlights are out, but the side lights are on” — when you see this combination, the culprit is between the light switch and the column lever: the white wire itself, the switch contacts, or a connector come loose. It's all within reach behind the dash, the shortest stretch in this story.
If instead the side lights are out too, the culprit is further back — the light switch itself or the black wire from the ignition switch's 30/4 (the last row of the cross-check below is that scene). ⚠️ Then turning the key changes nothing. This is where people who've come this far get most confused: it isn't broken because the key does nothing. The key never did anything there in the first place.

Once fixed

Switch on the lights,move the column lever,and the road lights up

Switch on the lights and push the column lever to II. The power that came along the white wire to the column lever passes to the low side, the single grey/red wire splits left and right in the fuse box, and both low beams light — with the key in position 1, or in position 2 turned left. After the repair, always check that both sides light on high beam, and that the indicator comes on. If you changed a bulb, the beam aim is worth checking too.

⭐ One last thing — the fault-finding chart in the factory service manual puts “short in the lighting circuit” at the top of the causes of “battery goes flat”. Ahead of leaving the lights on. A leak too small to blow the fuse drains a little every night without you noticing — if the battery is weaker every morning, this circuit is worth suspecting.

The wiring diagram shows only terminal names; when the column lever is moved, which terminal connects to which can't be read at the print resolution. This diagram shows it as “the power arriving on the white wire passes to either the low side or the high side” = the form in which the real car's behaviour holds (with the column lever at I the headlights don't light / low and high never light together). ⭐The three positions themselves are not an inference (factory owner's handbook = as explained under the picture above). ⛔ However, which contact is which terminal isn't written in the originals, and this diagram doesn't state which way the current flows either.
