The Charge Lamp Won't Go Out

Also called the charge warning light or battery lamp (the actual dash window is labelled "GENERAT."). We follow the electrical path on a single diagram to narrow down what's wrong.

This story is about the FIAT 500 (models D, F, L, R). The wiring is drawn from the factory diagrams for the 500.

The engine is running, but the small window at bottom left, GENERAT., stays lit red. That's the symptom for this page.

This page is about cars with the original dynamo (generator). Cars converted to an alternator follow a slightly different path → Alternator-conversion version
First, don't keep driving. On the 500, the fan belt turns both the generator and the cooling fan. The single most common reason the charge lamp stays lit is a belt problem — and if the belt has snapped, your cooling has stopped too. Pull over somewhere safe, switch off, open the engine lid, and check by eye whether the belt is in place, unbroken, and not slack. If it's snapped, don't drive on (no cooling means the engine will seize). If the belt's fine, read on.

Two touches of a multimeter narrow this down. But first, one minute on why this lamp lights up at all — understanding the logic makes the diagnosis much faster.

How it works

Why it lights with the key ON,and goes out once the engine runs

The charge lamp is a small bulb sitting in the middle of the electrical path.

  1. Key ON, engine stopped: battery → ignition switch → lamp → the coil inside the dynamo → chassis ground — a complete loop. Current flows = lit.
  2. Engine turning over: the dynamo starts generating, the contact inside the regulator (the cut-out) closes, and the same voltage as the top of the lamp now reaches the bottom too.
  3. When both ends reach the same voltage, no current flows = it goes out. In other words, "out" is the signal that the dynamo is charging.

Wire colours match the actual harness insulation (ROSSO=red, AZZURRO=light blue, VERDE=green, MARRONE=brown). Yellow dots = current flowing right now. Faint wire = no current there.

A common questionEven after the cut-out closes, the path to the lamp is still connected. It still doesn't light (and current doesn't flow backwards) because the top and bottom of the lamp are now tied to the same point at the same voltage — like two ponds at the same water level, nothing moves between them without a difference. "But shouldn't the dynamo side be higher while charging?" — that difference is absorbed by the thick charging path (contact → wiring → battery). The lamp sits in parallel with the closed contact, and a closed contact is nearly 0Ω — a shortcut that bypasses the lamp sitting next to it — so only a few dozen millivolts reach the lamp (nowhere near enough to glow). Conversely, if the regulator's setting drifts and the charging voltage wavers, a difference can appear and the lamp may glow faintly (the real cause of a "dimly lit" symptom).
When something's wrong

Lit while the engine is running =suspect the bottom half

If it's lit while turning over, the bottom of the lamp is still tied to ground — the charging voltage hasn't come up. Everything above the lamp, up through the key and instrument, is cleared; the suspects are only below it. In order of how often it happens:

  1. The belt isn't turning the dynamo (slack and slipping, or snapped — a belt can look fine and still not be tight enough)
  2. A fault in the dynamo itself (worn brushes, commutator, etc.)
  3. A faulty regulator (the cut-out contact won't close, or its setting has drifted)
  4. A wiring fault (the green wire, VERDE, or a ground wire chafed or disconnected)

It's tempting to suspect the fuse, but this lamp's path doesn't run through a fuse (terminal 30 at the ignition switch branches off ahead of fuse F1's supply side). Checking the fuse won't fix this symptom.

⭐The factory manuals even give a number for "how many RPM before it should go out" — roughly 1,100 rpm on the D, about 1,200 rpm on the L (around 23 km/h and 25 km/h respectively, in road speed). It's normal for it to stay lit at idle; if it goes out once you open the throttle a little, charging has started. ⚠️If it doesn't go out well above that speed, work through the four suspects above in order.

Narrowing it down

Two touches of a multimeter split the cause

This assumes you've already checked the belt by eye as described above (that's where a snapped or slipped belt gets caught). From here we use a multimeter to tell apart "not turning," "turning but not generating," and "generating but it's not getting through."

On the actual car

"Terminal 51" isright here on your car

You're measuring at the rear, in the engine bay. ● is the dynamo (beside the engine, on the left), ● is the regulator (on the inner wall, left side of the engine bay). "51" and "30" in the diagram above are terminals on these two parts.

Once it's fixed

Retensioned the belt.It went out.

It's not just out — you now know why it's out: the dynamo is turning, charging is back, the cut-out has closed, and the voltage difference across the lamp is gone.

You can also see the charging current returning to the battery on the right-hand path. It flows that way because, while charging, the dynamo side is at a higher voltage than the battery, and current flows from higher to lower. The cut-out is the gate that "opens this return path once charging starts, and disconnects it when it stops" (so the battery can't discharge backwards through the dynamo).

One more question people ask: "Doesn't the generated current just leak straight to the chassis through the dynamo's ground wire?" — It doesn't. Current only flows in a complete loop back to where it started, and the chassis ground is nothing more than a shared return conductor. The dynamo's positive side (terminal 51) is insulated from the chassis by its casing, so the only path is through the battery or the electrical loads. If the positive side ever did touch the chassis directly, that would be a short-circuit fault.
