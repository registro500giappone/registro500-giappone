How the Drivetrain Works

A read on what changes, and how, when you modify the drivetrain of an air-cooled FIAT 500 or 126 (final drive, 5-speed conversion, 3rd/4th short, long 1st gear, gearbox swap, tyres), told through both theory and how it feels. The purpose is to understand the structure of the drivetrain, not to recommend any modification.

0. How it works

When the tyre turns once, the car covers a distance of “outer diameter × π.” The stock 125R12 has an outer diameter of about 505mm, so one turn covers about 1.59m.

When the engine turns once, the car only covers part of that distance. That's because the gears and the final drive (the reduction unit) are cutting down the rotation, so the distance travelled per 1 engine turn works out to “the distance covered by 1 tyre turn ÷ (gear ratio × final-drive ratio).” The larger this ratio, the more times the engine has to turn to cover the same distance — that's called “short”; the smaller it is, the further the car travels per engine turn — that's called “long.”

A “long” gear lowers the revs at the same speed, but the force turning the tyres (driving force) drops by the same ratio. A “short” gear is the reverse. Every change to the drivetrain happens within this trade-off.

As a single ruler for comparing every change side by side, this page uses “the revs in the top gear while driving at 80km/h.” This isn't a measured value — it's calculated from the tooth counts and tyre specs (based on the 500F).

1. First, know your own stock values

A drivetrain change only makes sense once you know “from what, to what” it changes. First, let's line up the stock values by model. Revs at 80km/h are values worked out by this page's calculation core (the conversion formula from tooth counts and tyre outer diameter).

The 500's stock final drive is 8/41 = 5.125, the 126's is 8/39 = 4.875. 2. Changing the final drive

What changesThe final-drive ratio changes from 5.125 (8/41) to 4.875 (8/39), and every gear from 1st to 4th (and 5th too, if fitted) becomes uniformly about 5% longer.
Revs at 80km/h3887rpm → 3697rpm (−5%)
How it feelsAn account that with a stock 499cc engine, the 3rd-to-4th valley is easy to notice (499cc + 126 box build).

Try it on your own car (Drivetrain Daydream Simulator)

Changing the final drive (the final reduction unit, the coppia conica) stretches or shrinks the ratio of every gear uniformly, from 1st to 4th (and 5th too, if fitted). Compared with changing individual gears alone, this is a change that reaches the whole drivetrain, by structure.

In numbers: 8/41 (5.125) → 8/39 (4.875) gives −4.9% (about −5%). The 500F's revs at 80km/h drop from 3887 to 3697rpm. For 8/41 → 9/39 (4.333) it's −15.4% (about −15%), 3887 → 3286rpm.

9/39 does not fit the 500D's case (because the ring gear's outer diameter differs). It fits the 500F-onward and 126 cases without modification, by structure. Italian-language primary sources also state explicitly that pairing an 8/41 ring gear with a 126 pinion won't mesh — meaning a final drive only works as a ‘set matched to the case’.

Tooth counts of 9/41, 9/38, 10/39 and 10/37 also exist, but these are straight-cut teeth for dog-engagement (face-engaging) gearboxes, and no records of them being used for town driving have been found. This page treats these four as competition ratios and excludes them.

“Could cruise steadily at 60mph (about 97km/h) around 4000rpm”

A build with an engine tuned to 650cc + 126 gearbox + 9/39 (record from an English-language forum)

“1st gear is comically noisy”; “on a 20% gradient you need to change down”; “cruising at low revs cuts the fan's airflow, so even in winter the thermostat valve stayed almost fully closed”

A record from an owner who built a 650cc engine + 126 gearbox + 9/39 themselves (English-language forum)

A note as a structural consequence: because every gear becomes uniformly longer, by structure, this lowers cruising revs while also uniformly thinning out the force delivered on pull-away and on climbs. Sellers draw the line at 9/39 being for engines of “23CV or more,” and no records have been found of a stock 499cc engine paired with 9/39.

3. Adding a 5th gear

What changesA 5th gear (35/26 = 0.743) is added behind 4th, and 1st–4th stay as they are.
Revs at 80km/h3887rpm → 3300rpm (−15%) (cruising in 5th gear)
How it feels“It can cruise all day at 110km/h and pulls past 130km/h” (owner with a stock 650cc + 126 gearbox + 5-speed conversion)

Try it on your own car (Drivetrain Daydream Simulator)

A 5-speed conversion kit leaves the existing 1st–4th as they are and adds a 5th-gear pair at the rear of the gearbox (on the rear-cover side). The basic form is a ‘cantilever’ structure where the 5th-gear shaft is supported on only one side, and this is the starting point for the failure patterns described below.

In numbers: the most common ratio is 35/26 (0.743). Against the 500F's 4th gear of 0.875, revs at 80km/h drop from 3887 to 3300rpm (−15.1%, about −15%). Also sold commercially are a “long” group such as 27/19 (0.704), 36/26 (0.722) and 42/31 (0.738), and a “mid/short” group around 25/20–25/22 (ratios not so different from 4th gear).

Commercially sold 5-speed conversion kits effectively have a single manufacturer, and by structure each brand's product is the same model made under a different name. As exceptions, there are also designs that support the 5th-gear shaft at three points, or that add a bearing in the rear case to take the load, but most remain cantilevered.

Failure patterns (as a structural consequence)

  • ① Overload on the bearing under 5th gear: because of the cantilever structure, the bearing on the shaft carrying the 5th gear takes a heavier-than-normal load. German-language records report that “in nearly every known faulty gearbox, this bearing had failed,” with a figure of around 50,000km cited as a rough guide (this is the source's own stated figure, not something verified statistically).
  • ② Interference with the 2nd-gear shift fork: because the 5th-gear sleeve, as it moves back and forth, touches the 2nd-gear fork, by structure, the fork needs a chamfering operation. This is machining spelled out in the fitting instructions themselves, so it can't be fitted as a bolt-on.
  • ③ Assembly and manufacturing-batch variation: dents in the case, misaligned shift-rod holes, and other unit-to-unit hits and misses have been reported.

On the other hand, there are also several records of long, trouble-free use: “only replaced one bearing after 54,000km,” or “no problems over 25-plus years, or over 10 years of daily use.” Within what was researched, no cases of the shaft itself snapping, the case cracking, or oil leaks caused by the 5-speed conversion have been found.

“It can cruise all day at 110km/h and pulls past 130km/h. Before the 5-speed conversion, the engine was busy at 110km/h, and valve bounce meant it couldn't do more than 115km/h even downhill”

A UK owner with stock 650cc + 126 gearbox + 5-speed conversion

“No reliability problems after 10 years of daily use”

Owner with a 126 gearbox + 5-speed conversion (English-language forum). Cited as a counterpoint to the view that 5th gear should be limited to cruising only.

For fitting, it's reported that the following machining is needed: shortening the speedometer drive worm (records say the amount removed varies by model), chamfering the 2nd-gear fork (there's also a note that this has to be done on-site, at the final stage of assembly — if the fork is removed first, parts fall inside the case and a full strip-down becomes necessary), enlarging a hole at the top of the case, partially cutting away the reverse lockout, and shortening the rubber coupling on the shift linkage. Here is a summary of the steps following the fitting instructions:

  1. Drop the gearbox out, and remove the top cover and rear cover
  2. Remove the reverse fork's retaining bolt, engage both gears at once, and loosen the main-shaft and layshaft nuts
  3. Replace the 2nd gear, and machine the speedometer drive gear and the 2nd-gear hub
  4. Replace the reverse-selector rod with the new rod, and chamfer the 2nd-gear fork to match the 5th-gear sleeve's travel
  5. Pull the rear-cover studs, and enlarge the hole at the top of the case
  6. Fit the 5th gear together with its needle cage
  7. Tighten the main shaft and layshaft to the specified torque, and fit the locating set screw
  8. Fit the new rear cover and lever
  9. Modify the selector, fit the dedicated shift gate, and adjust it

This same sequence can be confirmed, almost identically and with photos, in a Minkara maintenance log found as a Japanese-language primary source (from 2017, fitted to a 500F).

A note as a structural consequence: because 5th is structurally “one gear added behind 4th,” the view is widely expressed that it should be treated as a cruising-only gear rather than one for everyday use (this is the flip side of the shaft being cantilevered). On the other hand, the fitting instructions give no guidance on changing the gearbox oil viscosity or on running it in.

4. Shortening 3rd/4th

What changes3rd changes from 1.300 to 1.368, 4th from 0.875 to 0.955 (NANNI, non-synchro box). 1st, 2nd and the final drive stay as they are.
Revs at 80km/h3887rpm → 4240rpm (+9%)
How it feels“The rev drops become even, so you're always within the power band” (a build combining 3rd/4th short with a long 5th gear; record from an English-language forum)

Try it on your own car (Drivetrain Daydream Simulator)

This swaps only 3rd and 4th for different tooth counts. 1st, 2nd and the final drive stay as they are; the aim is to narrow the rev drop (the “valley” between gears) when shifting from 3rd to 4th.

Even things called by the same name, “3rd/4th short,” come in two different lineages and are not the same thing inside.

  • NANNI type: 3rd 19/26 (1.368); 4th is 22 teeth (0.955) on the non-synchro box, 36/34 (0.944) on the synchro box. This ratio stands on its own, and by structure 4th alone still functions as a 4th gear for town driving.
  • Bacci type: 3rd 19/27 (1.421), 4th 22/24 (1.091). By structure, 4th is 25% shorter than the stock 0.875, and only becomes a top gear equivalent to stock 4th once a 5th of 0.880 (25/22) is added. On its own, 4th is too short — this is a structure that presupposes a 5th gear.

In numbers: on the 500F, 3rd goes 1.300 → 1.368 and 4th goes 0.875 → 0.955 (with NANNI, non-synchro box). Revs at 80km/h (comparing 4th alone) go 3887 → 4240rpm (+9.1%, about +9%). Top speed moves lower, by structure, since 4th gets shorter.

“The rev drops become even, so you're always within the power band”

A build combining 3rd/4th short with a long 5th gear (record from an English-language forum)

A note as a structural consequence: because the primary shaft (or the gears on it) is swapped as a whole, this is a job that requires dropping the gearbox out. It's also, by structure, a change that raises everyday revs across the board.

5. Lengthening 1st gear

What changesOnly 1st gear changes from 3.700 to 3.250 (the same tooth count as stock 126). 2nd–4th and the final drive stay as they are.
Revs at 80km/h3887rpm → 3887rpm (no change — since only 1st gear changes, it has no effect on 4th gear at 80km/h)
How it feelsThe body text notes explicitly that this change has fewer accounts describing how it feels compared with other changes, so confidence is lower.

Try it on your own car (Drivetrain Daydream Simulator)

This swaps only 1st gear for the same 12/39 (3.250) used in the stock 126. It's a longer ratio than the 500 non-synchro box's stock 1st gear of 10/37 (3.700), which narrows the drop from 1st to 2nd.

In numbers: 1st-gear ratio 3.700 → 3.250. By structure, the 1st-to-2nd drop (compared against 2nd gear's 2.067) narrows, and pulling away becomes correspondingly heavier.

For this change, we note explicitly that there are fewer accounts describing how it feels compared with other changes, so confidence is lower.

As a historical fact, the Abarth 595's homologated gear ratios used just a different, longer 1st gear of 11/36 (3.272), while 2nd–4th stayed stock 500. The idea itself of “changing only 1st gear” can also be seen in the Abarth's setup.

6. Swapping the whole gearbox

What changes1st gear changes from 3.700 to 3.250 (synchro box), and the final drive also changes from 8/41 to 8/39 — a side effect of swapping in the whole 126 gearbox.
Revs at 80km/h3887rpm → 3685rpm (−5%)
How it feelsThere are accounts that pairing a stock 499cc engine with the 126's 8/39 makes the “3rd-to-4th valley” feel pronounced.

Try it on your own car (Drivetrain Daydream Simulator)

This swaps the 500's non-synchro gearbox for a whole 126 synchro gearbox. There's no product sold as a dedicated conversion kit; it comes down to either sourcing a used 126 gearbox and transplanting the 500's bellhousing onto it, or combining the 126's bellhousing with its whole starter motor assembly.

Parts needed (as a matter of structure): the 500's own driveshafts (the 126's shafts are too long as they are); on early 500s (the generation with a narrower joint diameter), the rear wishbones also need swapping to match the joint diameter, by structure; and a clutch and flywheel matched to the input shaft's spline diameter (thicker on early cars, thinner on later ones).

Side effect: swapping the gearbox also changes the final drive to the 126's stock 8/39, by structure (fitting only the 126 gearbox while keeping 8/41 doesn't work, as long as the whole diff is being swapped). There are accounts that pairing a stock 499cc engine with the 126's 8/39 makes the “3rd-to-4th valley” feel pronounced.

As a countermeasure, there's also a documented approach that keeps the 126 gearbox's case as-is and swaps only the pinion for the 500's stock 8/41. However, this can't be achieved by swapping in the whole diff — it becomes an extra job of opening up the box and swapping the pinion alone.

As the reverse pattern, there's a Japanese-language record of keeping the 110F (500) gearbox as-is and swapping only the final drive for a 126A one. The pilot shaft's thick section is shared between the 110F and 126A, but the thin section is 6mm longer on the 126A, so the record's author speculates that “it might be possible by making a spacer 22mm in inner diameter and 6mm thick, and machining 6mm off the speedometer drive.” However, the author themselves writes this as “speculation,” and no confirmation from actual driving is recorded — so it's treated as an unverified account.

The gearbox oil specification splits by model along the lines of the structural difference: the 500 (non-synchro) calls for W90 (with EP additives), the 126 (synchro) for ZC90 (non-EP). The reason given in the records is that EP additives damage the brass parts in the synchro.

7. Changing the tyres

What changesTyre outer diameter shrinks from about 505mm to about 486mm (about −3.8%), so revs rise at the same speed. Gear ratios and the final drive stay stock.
Revs at 80km/h3887rpm → 4037rpm (+4%)
How it feelsA structural account that ride height drops by about −10mm and the speedometer reads higher than actual speed (there are also records that stock drum brakes don't interfere).

Try it on your own car (Drivetrain Daydream Simulator)

Outer diameter is set by “rim diameter (inches × 25.4mm) + 2 × (width × aspect ratio).” The stock 125/80R12 is about 505mm. Let's look at the change in outer diameter across four steps.

A note about 10-inch wheels: with the stock 500's PCD (4×190), the new wheels that still fit as-is are limited by structure. What's sold for town driving tends to be high-profile combinations like 145/80R10 or 165/70R10; low-profile 10-inch tyres remain rare in practice. Ride height drops by about −10mm, and the speedometer reads higher than actual speed, by structure. There are records that stock drum brakes don't interfere, while converting to disc brakes needs a dedicated kit.

A note about wide tyres (such as 145/70R12): they're about 2cm wider than the stock 125's width, and by structure stick out from the fender by about 1cm on each side. There are records of rubbing depending on wheel offset, or if a fender has a slight dent from an accident, and also records of dealing with it by lightly rolling the fender lip out of the way, or by cutting a notch into the fender line.

For 135/80R12, there is no explicit claim that it fits 3.5J wheels without modification, and there are records of slight rubbing at full lock. Ride height rises by about +8mm, by structure. Going to 13-inch presupposes switching to 5-inch-wide wheels, and for 155/65R13 there are several records of no interference. 135/80R13 is the stock size for the 126 BIS, built around a dedicated 4.5-inch-wide, 13-inch wheel. No records of it being fitted to the 500 have been found.

As a historical fact, the road-going Abarth 595/695 came stock with 12-inch wheels (steel, 3.5–4.5 inches wide; Campagnolo alloy wheels were an additional option). 10-inch wheels were an option on the competition-only 695SS Assetto Corsa model, fitted while keeping the drum brakes, by structure (the homologation form itself hasn't been confirmed, so this account is treated as coming from forum records).

A speedometer reading error is unavoidable, by structure, once the outer diameter changes, but no primary source has been found that quantifies it in something like a conversion table — so no figure has been made up here.

8. What happens when you combine changes

What changes3rd 19/27, 4th 22/24 (4th is 25% shorter than stock), plus a 5th of 0.880 added. The role of “4th” shifts to “5th”, and 3rd/4th are pushed toward climbing.
Revs at 80km/h3685rpm → 3718rpm (+1%) (baseline = 126 box + 8/39, cruising in 5th)
How it feelsNo individual account describing this exact combination has been found (this is a structural inference).

Try it on your own car (Drivetrain Daydream Simulator)

Let's look, as a matter of structure, at what happens when you stack several changes together (in every case, revs at 80km/h are calculated values based on the 500F).

  • (a) 126 gearbox + 8/41 pinion: this makes the gearbox synchro while putting only the pinion back to the stock 8/41. Revs at 80km/h are almost unchanged from stock 500 (about −0.3% by calculation). Only the shift feel becomes synchro, by structure; how the force is delivered barely changes. However, swapping the pinion alone requires the extra work of opening up the box.
  • (b) Bacci 3rd/4th short + 5th 0.88: this shortens 4th by 25% versus stock and adds a 5th of 0.880. Revs at 80km/h come out at about +0.6% of stock — almost back to the equivalent of stock 4th. The role of “4th” shifts to “5th,” and, by structure, 3rd and 4th get pushed toward climbing.
  • (c) NANNI 3rd/4th short + long 5th (0.743): this shortens 3rd and 4th while lengthening 5th. Revs at 80km/h come out at roughly −15% versus stock (−15.1% by calculation). It's a structure that stacks two changes — narrowing the valley between gears while also lowering cruising revs.
  • (d) 9/39 final drive + 5-speed conversion: this sets the final drive to 9/39 and then adds a 5th gear. Revs at 80km/h drop to roughly −28% versus stock (a calculated value). There's a structural point raised that the revs drop so far that the engine can't reach the rev range it needs, so top speed ends up not extending, or the car falls out of its everyday range.
  • (e) 10-inch wheels + 8/39 final drive: this pairs a change that shrinks the outer diameter by about 3.8% with a change that lengthens the final drive by about 4.9%, and the two cancel out. Revs at 80km/h come out almost the same as stock (about −1.2% by calculation). It ends up as a combination that changes only the look, while barely changing the rev characteristics.

9. Looking at it by situation

Here's how each change shows up, laid out by situation (in no particular order).

High-speed cruising

Lengthening the final drive (to 8/39, 9/39), adding a 5th gear, or switching to tyres with a larger outer diameter (12-inch larger diameter, 13-inch) — all of these work, by structure, to lower the revs. A 5th gear can lengthen cruising alone while leaving 1st–4th as they are; the final drive and tyres differ in that they act uniformly across every gear and the whole car, by structure.

Climbing

3rd/4th short (both the NANNI type and the Bacci type) narrows the valley between gears, by structure, which works to reduce the rev drop when climbing. Lengthening the final drive, or adding a 5th gear, works the opposite way on a climb, by structure — it thins out the force delivered.

Pulling away – 2nd gear

Lengthening 1st gear (to 12/39) narrows the 1st-to-2nd valley, by structure, but pulling away itself becomes heavier. There are fewer accounts describing how it feels, so confidence remains lower than for the other changes.

After raising displacement

Many accounts describe a relationship where raising the displacement makes it easier to draw out the benefit of lengthening the final drive or adding a 5th gear (records of lengthening the final drive with the stock displacement remain scarce in themselves). If the gearbox is swapped for a 126 gearbox, the final drive changes to 8/39 as a side effect, by structure.

Synchro or not

There is a structural difference: the 500's stock gearbox is non-synchro, the 126's stock gearbox is synchro. The only way to get synchro while keeping a 500 is to swap in a whole 126 gearbox; there is no product sold as a dedicated conversion kit.

Tyre look

The change in outer diameter shows up in the revs too, by structure, for 10-inch (smaller), and 12-inch larger diameter / 13-inch (larger); but 12-inch same-diameter (such as 145/70R12) only changes the width, and barely shows up in the revs, by structure.

10. Related structures

Speedometer drive: changing the final drive or 5th-gear setup comes with machining to shorten the speedometer drive's worm gear, by structure (records say the amount removed varies by model). No primary source has been found offering something like a conversion table between revs and the speedometer reading.

Gearbox oil specification: the stock specification is W90 (with EP additives) for the 500 (non-synchro), and ZC90 (non-EP) for the 126 (synchro). The reason given in the records is that EP additives damage the brass parts in the synchro (see also section 6).

Mounts: the parts that fix the engine and gearbox to the body — at the front, springs, bushes and arms absorb vibration; at the rear, a traversa (crossmember) and rubber tassels do, by structure. Since changing the drivetrain changes how force is delivered, the general view is to also treat the mounts as a wear item (no primary source has been found showing the increased load or replacement interval after a modification, so no figure is given here).

Reinforced clutch, LSD (limited-slip differential), reinforced driveshaft: all of these exist as products, but no primary source in any record has been found showing the threshold of “from what power output they're needed.” This page excludes them from its scope.

11. Sources and the limits of this page

The only thing the calculations can give you is “revs at the same speed.” Failures, cooling, noise and fuel consumption have not been quantified. The impressions quoted as accounts are individual records, not statistical verification, each noted with who said it and for what build.

The table of speeds at 4000rpm in the fiatforum thread “Gearbox best option” is suspected of having been calculated using a 4th-gear ratio of 1.00 (about 10% lower than the same forum's own measured values and figures worked out from the specs), so it isn't used on this page.

Primary sources that couldn't be read: Axel Gerstl's (webshop.fiat500126.com) product pages couldn't be opened directly (403), so they were checked through the listing information (product names, fitment notes) collected by our own site's parts search. Three final-drive types are listed — 8/41 (500/126), 8/39 (126), and 9/39 (126P/BIS, the long ratio) — along with 5-speed conversion parts (the shift-rail-side housing and gate) and 12-inch tyres (135/80, 145/70). No gear set with the tooth counts stated is listed. Many 500forum.it threads sit behind a login wall, and only excerpts that could be confirmed are treated as “accounts.” Almost every thread on 500forum.de returned a connection error, so only search-engine excerpts are referenced.
